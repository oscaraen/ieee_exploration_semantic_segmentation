\begin{abstract}
This paper reviews the six most frequently cited models of the modern semantic
segmentation literature: the fully convolutional network, U-Net, SegNet, DeepLab,
the pyramid scene parsing network, and DeepLabv3+. For each model we summarise the
techniques its authors applied and the results they reported, working from the
primary arXiv sources rather than from secondary summaries. Two questions organise
the review. First, what mechanism does each architecture use to reconcile the two
competing demands of dense prediction, namely the long-range context needed to
decide what a region is and the spatial precision needed to decide where it begins?
We find four successive answers: learned upsampling with skip fusion, symmetric
encoder-decoder recovery, explicit resolution control through atrous convolution and
multi-scale context aggregation, and finally the deliberate combination of pyramid
pooling with a decoder. Second, what do the reported numbers actually license? We
reproduce the authors' own figures with their datasets and metrics attached, and show
that the headline scores span different benchmarks, different backbones, different
pre-training corpora and different inference protocols, so that the apparent
monotone progression from 62.2 to 89.0 percent conflates architectural gain with
data and compute. We also find that the efficiency claims are mutually
incommensurable: three of the six papers report cost in units the others do not use
at all. We close by identifying gaps in boundary evaluation, reporting discipline,
annotation cost and deployment under constrained memory.
\end{abstract}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{emantic} segmentation assigns a category label to every pixel in
an image, producing a dense, structured description of a scene rather than a single
image-level decision. Because the output lives at the resolution of the input, every
segmentation model must satisfy two demands at once: it must aggregate long-range
semantic context in order to decide \emph{what} a region is, and it must preserve
fine spatial detail in order to decide precisely \emph{where} that region begins and
ends. Classification backbones are built to discard exactly the information the
second demand requires --- repeated striding and pooling reduce spatial resolution by
a factor of 32 in each direction --- so the central design problem of the field is
how to recover it. Almost every architectural idea reviewed here can be read as a
particular answer to that one problem.

This paper reviews the six works that the field cites most heavily, and which
together define the problem's canonical answers. The fully convolutional network
(FCN) of Long \emph{et al.}~\cite{long2014fully} reframed segmentation as per-pixel
classification, showed that a classification backbone could be fine-tuned end-to-end
from pixels to pixels, and recovered resolution by learning the upsampling and fusing
predictions from shallower layers. U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} made the
recovery path symmetric with the contraction path and concatenated full-resolution
encoder features into it, an arrangement designed for the small, heavily augmented
datasets of biomedical microscopy. SegNet~\cite{badrinarayanan2015segnet} kept the
symmetric decoder but replaced the transferred feature maps with the max-pooling
indices alone, trading a little accuracy for an order-of-magnitude reduction in the
memory the decoder must hold at inference. DeepLab~\cite{chen2016deeplab} attacked
the problem earlier in the pipeline, using atrous convolution to control the
resolution at which features are computed in the first place, adding atrous spatial
pyramid pooling for multi-scale context and a fully connected conditional random
field (CRF) to sharpen boundaries. The pyramid scene parsing network
(PSPNet)~\cite{zhao2016pspnet} diagnosed the FCN's residual failures as failures of
global context and aggregated context over a four-level spatial pyramid.
DeepLabv3+~\cite{chen2018encoderdecoder} closed the loop, combining the pyramid
pooling lineage with the encoder-decoder lineage and making both cheap through atrous
separable convolution.

Our aim is synthesis rather than enumeration, and the review is deliberately
two-sided. Section~\ref{sec:sota} presents the state of the art: it states the scope
and inclusion criteria, groups the six works into thematic clusters by the mechanism
each uses, summarises the techniques applied, reports the results the authors
themselves obtained with the dataset and metric attached to each figure, traces the
methodological trends that connect them, separates consensus from unresolved
disagreement, and identifies gaps and future directions.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{State of the Art}
\label{sec:sota}

\subsection{Scope and Inclusion Criteria}

This review covers learning-based architectures for dense semantic labelling of
images. A work was included if it satisfied three criteria. First, it had to
introduce a general-purpose segmentation \emph{architecture}, not an application of
an existing one to a particular domain. Second, it had to report quantitative results
on at least one standard public benchmark, so that its claims could be placed on a
common axis. Third, it had to be among the most heavily cited works in the field, on
the evidence of standard bibliometric consensus.

That third criterion requires an explicit caveat, because it is the weakest link in
the selection. Citation counts are not published by the preprint archive from which
the primary sources were retrieved, and the citation-graph service consulted for this
review returned a rate-limit error rather than counts. The six works listed in
Table~\ref{tab:models} are therefore selected as the field's most-cited models on the
basis of bibliometric consensus rather than counts re-verified for this paper, and no
citation figures are quoted anywhere in what follows. Readers should treat the
ranking as a reasonable identification of the canonical set, not as a measurement.

Two boundaries of scope should also be stated plainly. Instance and panoptic
segmentation are out of scope, which excludes some very heavily cited work --- Mask
R-CNN most notably --- whose object of study is the individuated instance rather than
the semantic region. And the evidence reviewed here is drawn entirely from
author-reported results, which have not been independently re-run; this is a
limitation we return to in Section~\ref{subsec:disagree}.

\subsection{Thematic Clusters in Prior Work}

The six works fall into four clusters, each defined by the mechanism it uses to
reconcile semantic context with spatial precision.

\subsubsection{Reframing Dense Prediction}
The first cluster contains a single work, because the FCN's contribution was a
reframing rather than a mechanism. Long \emph{et al.} observe that convolution,
pooling and the non-linearities all operate on local regions and depend only on
relative coordinates, so a network composed solely of such layers accepts inputs of
arbitrary size and emits correspondingly sized outputs~\cite{long2014fully}. Fully
connected layers are reinterpreted as convolutions whose kernels cover their entire
input region, which converts a classification network into a dense predictor at the
cost of a coarse output stride. Two consequences follow. The first is efficiency:
amortising computation over overlapping receptive fields, the authors report that
producing a $10\times10$ grid of outputs from a $500\times500$ image takes 22\,ms
against 1.2\,ms for a single $227\times227$ classification, more than five times
faster than the naive patchwise approach. The second is the problem the rest of the
field inherits, since a stride of 32 pixels bounds the achievable detail.

The FCN's own answer has two parts. Upsampling is performed in-network by backwards
convolution, which is trivial to implement because it simply reverses the forward and
backward passes of convolution, and whose filters need not be fixed to bilinear
interpolation but can be learned. And the ``skip'' architecture fuses predictions from
shallower, finer layers into the coarse prediction by summation, turning the linear
topology into a directed acyclic graph. The authors are careful about why summation
rather than maximum: max fusion made learning difficult through gradient switching.
Each refinement stage is initialised from the coarser network with the new parameters
zeroed so that training begins from unmodified predictions, with the learning rate cut
by a factor of 100.

\subsubsection{Symmetric Encoder--Decoder Recovery}
The second cluster accepts the coarse-to-fine framing but makes the recovery path a
full mirror of the contraction path. U-Net's expansive path halves the channel count
at each step with an up-convolution, concatenates the correspondingly cropped
encoder feature map, and applies two $3\times3$ convolutions; because the large
channel count is retained in the upsampling half, context information propagates to
the high-resolution layers~\cite{ronneberger2015unet}. The design is adapted
throughout to the scarcity of biomedical annotation. Unpadded convolutions mean the
output contains only pixels for which full context was available, which in turn
permits seamless tiling of arbitrarily large images with mirrored extrapolation at the
borders. Elastic deformation is the central augmentation, generated from random
displacements on a coarse $3\times3$ grid sampled from a Gaussian with a standard
deviation of 10 pixels and interpolated bicubically. And the loss is weighted per
pixel by $w(x)=w_c(x)+w_0\exp(-(d_1(x)+d_2(x))^2/2\sigma^2)$ with $w_0=10$ and
$\sigma\approx5$ pixels, where $d_1$ and $d_2$ are the distances to the nearest and
second-nearest cell borders, so that the network is forced to learn the narrow
separations between touching cells. Memory is spent on large input tiles rather than
large batches, the batch reduced to a single image and compensated by a momentum of
0.99.

SegNet keeps the symmetry but attacks what the decoder has to remember. Its encoder is
topologically identical to the 13 convolutional layers of VGG16, and discarding the
fully connected layers reduces the encoder from 134\,M to 14.7\,M parameters. The
novelty is in the upsampling: only the max-pooling indices are memorised, in principle
two bits per $2\times2$ window, and the decoder scatters its input back to those
positions and densifies the result with a trainable filter
bank~\cite{badrinarayanan2015segnet}. Learning to upsample is thereby eliminated
entirely. The authors quantify the saving directly against FCN-Basic: storing 64
feature maps of the first layer at $180\times240$ in 32-bit floating point costs
11\,MB, the dimensionality-reduced form costs about 1.9\,MB, and the pooling indices
cost 0.17\,MB. Their ablation over decoder variants is the most systematic in the set,
and it yields three stated rules: storing the encoder maps in full performs best,
especially on boundary measures; compressed forms used with a suitable decoder
recover most of that performance when inference memory is constrained; and larger
decoders help for a given encoder.

\subsubsection{Resolution Control and Multi-Scale Context}
The third cluster intervenes before resolution is lost. DeepLab localises the last
resolution-reducing layer, sets its stride to one, and replaces subsequent
convolutions with atrous convolutions, $y[i]=\sum_k x[i+r\cdot k]\,w[k]$, where the
rate $r$ sets the input sampling stride~\cite{chen2016deeplab}. Because inserting
$r-1$ zeros between filter taps gives a $k\times k$ filter an effective size of
$k+(k-1)(r-1)$, the field of view can be enlarged without adding parameters or
computation. The authors adopt a hybrid: atrous convolution quadruples feature
density to one response every 8 pixels, and fast bilinear interpolation covers the
remaining factor of 8, since computing responses at full image resolution ``ends up
being too costly'' and the score maps are smooth enough that interpolation suffices.
Atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) then probes one feature layer with several
parallel atrous branches at different rates, the explicit contrast being with the
serial arrangement of dilated layers used elsewhere. Finally a fully connected CRF
with a bilateral and a spatial Gaussian kernel, solved by ten mean-field iterations
accelerated by permutohedral-lattice filtering, refines the boundaries. The CRF is a
post-process: the authors state plainly that they decouple the two training stages and
hold the unary terms fixed when setting the CRF parameters.

PSPNet starts from an error analysis rather than an architecture. Its authors catalogue
three failure modes of the FCN on ADE20K --- mismatched relationships, where a boat is
labelled a car despite cars rarely appearing on rivers; confusion categories, where
field and earth or building and skyscraper are conflated; and inconspicuous classes,
where small objects are missed and large ones exceed the effective receptive field and
come out discontinuous~\cite{zhao2016pspnet}. The common factor they identify is
missing global context, and they note the empirical receptive field of a CNN is much
smaller than its theoretical one. Their pyramid pooling module aggregates context at
bin sizes of $1\times1$, $2\times2$, $3\times3$ and $6\times6$, applies a $1\times1$
convolution after each level to reduce the context representation to $1/N$ of its
dimension so that the global feature does not dominate, upsamples bilinearly and
concatenates. The contrast with a single global average pooling vector is explicit:
collapsing everything into one vector can lose spatial relation and introduce
ambiguity. A second classifier after the res4b22 block of ResNet101 provides deep
supervision with a balance weight $\alpha=0.4$, discarded at test time.

\subsubsection{Convergence of the Two Lineages}
The fourth cluster is the deliberate merger of the second and third. DeepLabv3+ frames
the field as a choice between spatial pyramid pooling, which encodes multi-scale
context, and encoder-decoder structures, which recover sharp boundaries, and refuses
the choice: DeepLabv3 becomes the encoder and a simple decoder is appended
\cite{chen2018encoderdecoder}. The decoder upsamples the encoder output by four,
concatenates low-level backbone features whose channels have first been reduced by a
$1\times1$ convolution --- necessary because 256 or 512 low-level channels would
otherwise outweigh the encoder's 256 and impede training --- refines with a few
$3\times3$ convolutions, and upsamples by four again. The authors also supply the
clearest statement in the set of why pure dilation does not scale: with ResNet-101, an
output stride of 16 requires dilating 9 layers, but an output stride of 8 affects 26
residual blocks, or 78 layers, which is computationally prohibitive. Their remedy is
atrous separable convolution, factorising each convolution into a depthwise atrous
spatial convolution and a pointwise combination, applied to both ASPP and the decoder.

\subsection{Techniques Applied: A Synthesis}

Read across the six works, the techniques resolve into five recurring devices.

\emph{Transfer from classification.} Every model begins from an ImageNet-pre-trained
backbone and fine-tunes all layers. The FCN's authors quantify the dependence: tuning
the output classifier alone yields barely 70 percent of the performance of full
fine-tuning, and training from scratch is not viable. SegNet's encoder is chosen to be
topologically identical to VGG16 precisely so that the pre-trained weights transfer.

\emph{Resolution recovery.} The four distinct answers are learned backwards
convolution with summed skips (FCN), concatenation of cropped encoder features into a
symmetric path (U-Net), scattering by memorised pooling indices (SegNet), and
preventing the loss in the first place through atrous convolution with a terminal
bilinear step (DeepLab, PSPNet, DeepLabv3+). DeepLabv3+ is the only work to combine
two of them.

\emph{Multi-scale context.} ASPP and the pyramid pooling module are the same idea
realised differently: probe one feature layer at several scales in parallel and fuse.
DeepLab's branches differ in atrous rate, with configurations $r=\{6,12,18,24\}$ for
ASPP-L; PSPNet's differ in pooling bin size. DeepLab's authors also evaluate the
alternative of running parallel branches over rescaled inputs with max fusion, and
reject it as requiring feature responses at all layers for multiple input scales.

\emph{Boundary treatment.} Three strategies appear, and they are not equivalent.
DeepLab appends a dense CRF as a decoupled post-process. DeepLabv3+ makes boundary
refinement architectural and internal via the decoder, and measures the result in a
trimap band around the ground-truth boundary. U-Net makes it a loss-function problem,
upweighting the thin separations between touching instances.

\emph{Training discipline.} The poly learning-rate policy, with the rate scaled by
$(1-\mathrm{iter}/\mathrm{max\_iter})^{0.9}$, is adopted by DeepLab, PSPNet and
DeepLabv3+, and DeepLab measures its contribution in isolation. Class balancing
divides the set: SegNet uses median frequency balancing, weighting each class by the
ratio of the median class frequency to its own, while the FCN's authors find balancing
unnecessary even though roughly three quarters of their pixels are background.
PSPNet's authors stress that batch-normalisation batch size matters enough that they
modified Caffe to synchronise statistics across GPUs via OpenMPI to reach a batch of
16.

\subsection{Results Reported by the Authors}

Table~\ref{tab:results} collects the principal figures with their datasets and
metrics attached. Four observations follow that the table alone does not make.

First, the progression on PASCAL VOC 2012 test --- 62.2, 79.7, 85.4 and 89.0 percent
mean IoU for FCN, DeepLab, PSPNet and DeepLabv3+ --- is not a clean measure of
architectural progress, because the later figures depend on additional data. PSPNet
reports 82.6 percent when trained on VOC 2012 alone and 85.4 percent with MS-COCO
pre-training; its authors note pointedly that the VOC-only figure already exceeds
prior methods that did use COCO. DeepLabv3+ reports 87.8 percent with Xception and
89.0 percent only with additional JFT-300M pre-training, a corpus that is not public.

Second, the ablations are more informative than the headline numbers, and they are
remarkably consistent in magnitude. DeepLab's dense CRF yields a 3 to 5 percent
absolute increase in mean IoU across model variants in the conference-version
experiments, and 1.34 points on top of the final ResNet-101 configuration, whose val
ablation runs 68.72, 71.27, 73.28, 74.87, 75.54, 76.35 and 77.69 percent as
multi-scale input, COCO pre-training, augmentation, LargeFOV, ASPP and the CRF are
added in turn. PSPNet's pyramid pooling module contributes 4.45 points of mean IoU and
2.03 of pixel accuracy over a ResNet50 baseline on ADE20K val, taking 37.23/78.01 to
41.68/80.04, while its auxiliary loss contributes 1.41 and 0.94 points at
$\alpha=0.4$; average pooling beats max pooling at every setting tested, and the
four-level pyramid beats single global pooling by 1.61 points. DeepLabv3+'s decoder
moves 77.21 to 78.85 percent at an evaluation output stride of 16, for about 20\,B
extra multiply-adds, and improves the trimap band around boundaries by 4.8 points with
ResNet-101 and 5.4 with Xception, which is the only direct measurement of boundary
gain in the set.

Third, the efficiency claims cannot be compared, because the six papers report cost in
units that do not overlap. The FCN reports forward time per image, 210\,ms for
FCN-VGG16 at $500\times500$ and roughly 175\,ms for FCN-8s at inference, against about
50\,s for the prior state of the art, a speedup it quantifies as 114-fold for the
convolutional network alone and 286-fold overall. SegNet reports GPU memory and model
size: 1052\,MB of inference memory against 1806 to 1993\,MB for the alternatives, and
117\,MB of model against 539\,MB for FCN and 877\,MB for DeconvNet, while being
roughly four times slower than DeepLab-LargeFOV in the forward pass at 422.50 against
110.06\,ms. DeepLab reports 8 frames per second for the dense network plus about 0.5\,s
of CPU time per image for the CRF, which means the post-process dominates total
latency. DeepLabv3+ reports multiply-adds, from 52.43\,B to 5247.07\,B across its
configurations, and notes that separable convolution cuts them by 33 to 41 percent at
similar accuracy. U-Net reports 10 hours of training on a 6\,GB Titan and under one
second per $512\times512$ image. PSPNet reports no timing at all.

Fourth, two of the six report results that are not on natural-image scene benchmarks
at all, and they are the strongest relative margins in the set. U-Net took first place
on the ISBI 2012 EM challenge ranked by warping error, at 0.000353 against 0.000355 for
the runner-up, and won the ISBI 2015 cell tracking challenge with mean IoU of 0.9203 on
PhC-U373 against 0.83 for the second-best method and 0.7756 on DIC-HeLa against 0.46 ---
a margin of more than 30 points. Its authors are candid that on the EM task they do not
lead on the other two metrics: their Rand error of 0.0382 trails DIVE-SCI's 0.0305, and
their pixel error of 0.0611 trails DIVE's 0.0582.

\subsection{Methodological Trends Over Time}

Three trends are visible across the four years the six works span.

The first is a migration of the resolution problem from the decoder to the encoder.
FCN, U-Net and SegNet all accept a heavily strided encoder and invest in machinery to
undo it; DeepLab onwards change the encoder so that less is lost. The migration is
not free, and DeepLabv3+ states its price in layers dilated. That the field then
returned a decoder to the atrous encoder is the clearest evidence that neither
intervention alone is sufficient.

The second is the steady displacement of hand-designed components by learned or
architectural ones. The FCN replaces fixed bilinear upsampling with learned
deconvolution; SegNet removes the learning again but only by exploiting information
the encoder already computed; DeepLab's CRF is a classical model bolted to a learned
one, and DeepLabv3+ replaces that external refinement with an internal decoder.
Boundary quality thus moves from post-processing to architecture over the period, and
DeepLab's authors anticipate this move themselves, conjecturing that an
encoder-decoder structure of the SegNet or U-Net kind could alleviate their boundary
failures and leaving it as future work.

The third is the growing dominance of data and compute over architecture. Later works
report their best numbers only with MS-COCO or JFT-300M pre-training, and the
increments from pre-training --- about 2 points for COCO and 0.8 to 1 for JFT in
DeepLabv3+'s ablation --- are comparable to or larger than the increments from the
architectural contributions those same papers are about.

\subsection{Consensus Findings and Unresolved Disagreements}
\label{subsec:disagree}

Several findings are established across independent groups. Fine-grained encoder
information must reach the output in some form: every work in the set either
transfers it explicitly or controls resolution to avoid needing to, and SegNet's
ablation shows that the fully learned-free alternative, fixed bilinear upsampling,
is worst on every metric it measures. Multi-scale context aggregation helps, whether
realised as atrous branches or pooling bins, and both papers that introduce it measure
a gain of the same order. Supervised pre-training on classification is necessary rather
than merely convenient. And average pooling beats max fusion for aggregation, a
conclusion PSPNet reaches by ablation and the FCN reaches independently from the
gradient-switching difficulties of max fusion in its skip connections.

Three disagreements remain genuinely open, and we state them separately because the
evidence does not settle them.

The first is comparability, and it is severe in this set. The six papers report on
different benchmarks with different metrics: SegNet reports a boundary F1 measure that
no other paper in the set uses, U-Net reports warping and Rand errors that none of the
scene-parsing papers use, and the FCN reports four variants of accuracy where later
works report mean IoU alone. Where the benchmarks do coincide, the protocols do not.
DeepLab's 79.7 percent, PSPNet's 85.4 percent and DeepLabv3+'s 89.0 percent on VOC
2012 test differ in backbone, pre-training corpus, multi-scale inference and
flipping, and the papers themselves disclose this in ablation tables that abstracts do
not carry. A reader comparing abstracts alone would attribute to architecture what is
substantially attributable to data.

The second concerns the accuracy--efficiency frontier, where there is not even an
agreed unit. As the preceding subsection sets out, the six papers report
latency, memory, model size, multiply-adds and training time in non-overlapping
combinations. SegNet is explicit that its advantage appears only ``when inference time
memory is constrained but where inference time can be compromised to some extent'',
which is a frank admission that it loses on the axis its competitors report.
DeepLabv3+, conversely, deliberately ignores computational cost for its test-set
configuration. These are different objectives presented in the same field.

The third concerns whether architectural components generalise across domains, and here
the evidence is directly contradictory. DeepLab's authors observe no improvement
whatsoever from either LargeFOV or ASPP on PASCAL-Person-Part. DeepLabv3+ finds that
the image-level features of ASPP help on VOC 2012 but that removing them improves
Cityscapes. SegNet finds that DeepLab-LargeFOV, the smallest model it benchmarks,
attains slightly better mIoU than SegNet on SUN RGB-D at 32.08 against 31.84, and uses
this to argue that heavier parameterisation does not guarantee better performance.
Multi-scale context modules are therefore not unconditionally beneficial, and no work
in the set offers a predictive account of when they will help.

\subsection{Gaps and Open Research Questions}

Four gaps follow from the preceding analysis.

\emph{Boundary evaluation.} Mean IoU is a region-overlap measure relatively
insensitive to boundary error, and two papers in the set say so from opposite
directions. The FCN's authors show by downsampling and re-upsampling the ground truth
that mean IoU of 86.1 percent is attainable at a stride of 32 and 92.8 at 16,
concluding that pixel-perfect prediction is clearly not necessary to score far above
the state of the art and that mean IoU is therefore not a good measure of fine-scale
accuracy. SegNet's authors reach the same conclusion and respond by adding a boundary
F1 measure. That measure was not adopted by the later works, so the field's principal
metric remains insensitive to exactly the property that DeepLabv3+'s decoder and
DeepLab's CRF were built to improve.

\emph{Reporting discipline.} The comparability problem is methodological rather than
technical and is the most tractable of the four. What is missing is a convention
pinning backbone, pre-training corpus, crop size, schedule length, output stride and
single- versus multi-scale inference to every reported score. Without it, measured
progress and measured compute cannot be separated.

\emph{Annotation cost and class imbalance.} The results on SUN RGB-D are poor for every
architecture benchmarked, and SegNet's authors attribute this to 37 classes of which
many occupy little area and appear rarely, reporting accuracies of 0.00 for floor mat
and 0.03 for shower curtain. They conjecture that VGG-derived architectures may be
unable to absorb the variability of indoor scenes but note this would need more
controlled datasets to verify. U-Net's weighted loss and SegNet's median frequency
balancing are two different partial answers, and the FCN's finding that balancing is
unnecessary on VOC shows the problem is dataset-dependent rather than solved.

\emph{Deployment under constrained memory.} SegNet is the only work in the set that
treats inference memory as a first-class objective, and the frontier it describes ---
accuracy against memory, at the cost of latency --- was not pursued by its successors,
who optimised accuracy and reported cost after the fact. No systematic account exists
of what accuracy is forfeited at a given memory budget.

\subsection{Suggested Future Directions}

Three directions follow directly. The first is a shared protocol for boundary-aware
evaluation, since both metrics proposed in this set were abandoned by later work and
the property they measure is the one several of the architectures exist to improve.
The second is a predictive account of when multi-scale context modules help, grounded
in properties of the label distribution and object scale statistics rather than
benchmark-by-benchmark ablation; the contradictory findings on Person-Part,
Cityscapes and SUN RGB-D are the natural test set for such an account. The
third is the disentangling of architecture from pre-training, which would require
re-running the later models under matched data budgets --- an experiment none of the
original papers performs, and which the field's reliance on non-public corpora such as
JFT-300M makes difficult to perform independently.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Across the four years separating the fully convolutional network from DeepLabv3+,
semantic segmentation did not refine a single idea so much as relocate one problem.
The problem is the recovery of spatial resolution that classification backbones are
built to discard, and the six most-cited models of the field are best read as four
successive places to solve it: in the decoder by learning to upsample and fusing
shallow predictions, in a symmetric expansion path that carries encoder features or
merely their pooling indices, in the encoder itself by controlling the sampling rate
with atrous convolution, and finally in both at once. The reviewed evidence supports
that relocation clearly. It supports far less clearly the headline comparisons through
which the progression is usually narrated, because the later scores rest on
pre-training corpora and inference protocols that the abstracts do not disclose and
that the papers' own ablations show to contribute increments comparable to the
architectures being proposed. Two of these papers also demonstrate, by their own
measurements, that the field's principal metric is insensitive to the boundary quality
several of these architectures were built to deliver. The most useful near-term
contribution to this literature may therefore not be a new architecture but a shared
discipline for measuring the ones that already exist.
